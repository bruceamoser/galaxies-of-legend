# Build a sector worth crossing

Start with a small connected region, not a galaxy map. Five places with reasons to visit are enough to begin design work: a working port, an industrial site, a settled world, an uncertain frontier location and a route or relay whose reliability matters.

## Make each place actionable

For each location write what the crew can obtain, what it costs to remain, who controls access and what is changing. Give it an ordinary activity: ore processing, repair, farming, research, passenger transfer or salvage brokerage. A station exists between adventures; workers have shifts, supplies have routes and doors have owners.

| Record | Useful question |
|---|---|
| Arrival | What can the crew see, hear and contact before committing? |
| Need | What does this place import, repair, hide or lack? |
| Access | Who can grant entry, dock space, information or work? |
| Pressure | What will change if nobody intervenes? |
| Routes | Where can the crew go next, and what limits the connection? |
| Evidence | What three concrete facts can reveal what is happening? |

Avoid adding a culture, species or law solely to justify a single encounter. An unfamiliar community can have a motive and a communication problem before its ancestry mechanics exist. Record any physiology-dependent ruling as unresolved until Bruce's ancestry material supplies it.

## Connect locations honestly

Use a route diagram with distances in the Starship Manual's abstract route units. Distance is not automatically danger. State known hazards, chart quality and destinations before the crew buys fuel or begins preparation. A longer route needs the range and charge capacity printed in that book, not a harder personal roll that manufactures missing hardware.

A local shuttle can still provide a useful session around a world or station. Owning no FTL drive does not remove local choices. A carrier, paid passage or an alliance can change travel access without granting the crew a free permanent ship upgrade.

## Factions need capabilities and limits

Write a faction's goal, leverage, constraint, public face and next move. A mining cooperative can delay fuel but may be unable to enforce a threat outside its docks. A survey office may know the route but need the crew to verify a signal. Give factions something they want from one another so the crew can negotiate between them.

| d6 | Goal | Useful leverage | Limitation |
|---:|---|---|---|
| 1 | Keep a settlement supplied | Stores, schedules, local trust | Thin reserve stock |
| 2 | Control a profitable route | Charts and pilots | Needs outside repairs |
| 3 | Recover disputed property | Records and witnesses | Cannot prove ownership |
| 4 | Protect a vulnerable workforce | Solidarity and access | Cannot survive a long shutdown |
| 5 | Conceal a costly mistake | Institutional permissions | Evidence already travelled |
| 6 | Establish a frontier claim | A ship and a plausible map | No reliable local support |

These are preparation prompts, not mechanical bonuses or a random mandate to the players. Choose or roll when useful; combine results only when they make sense.

## Three leads instead of one gate

For an important conclusion, identify at least three available leads. A damaged cargo seal, a shift record and a witness can all point toward a diverted shipment. The crew's method decides which it finds and what it risks, not whether a single missed roll locks the whole story.

A lead can move: the witness goes off shift, the manifest is copied, the cargo leaves dock. Show these changes when time matters. Do not erase every clue because the crew chose a different route or talked to the wrong person first.

## Keep a sector ledger

After each session, update established facts, faction reactions, route changes, damaged facilities, obligations and unanswered questions. Separate a rumour from a confirmed fact. Let a visible change follow an earlier choice; avoid secretly adjusting every faction to keep the crew on a prepared path.
