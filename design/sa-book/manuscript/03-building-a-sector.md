# Build a sector worth crossing

Put one working port on the page. Add an industrial site that supplies it, a settled world that depends on it, a frontier site that draws people away and a route or relay that ties them together. Five places with reasons to visit are enough to begin.

## Make each place actionable

For each location write what the crew can obtain, what it costs to remain, who controls access and what is changing. Give it an ordinary activity: ore processing, repair, farming, research, passenger transfer or salvage brokerage. A station exists between adventures; workers have shifts, supplies have routes and doors have owners.

| Record | Useful question |
|---|---|
| Arrival | What can the crew see, hear and contact before committing? |
| Need | What does this place import, repair, hide or lack? |
| Access | Who can grant entry, dock space, information or work? |
| Pressure | What will change if nobody intervenes? |
| Routes | Where can the crew go next, and what limits the connection? |
| Evidence | What three concrete facts can reveal what is happening? |

Give each community a reason to be there and a reason to care about the route. Player’s Book Appendix A gives you ten systems with ports and arrival problems. Use the published ancestry traits when physiology matters in play.

## Connect locations honestly

Label known routes with distance, chart quality and hazards. Give the crew those facts before they spend fuel. Use Starship Manual Chapter 4 for range, charge and travel. Ordinary travel only calls for a check at a consequential obstacle. A crew without FTL can take local work, arrange paid passage or find a carrier.

## Factions need capabilities and limits

Write a faction's goal, leverage, constraint, public face and next move. A mining cooperative can delay fuel but may be unable to enforce a threat outside its docks. A survey office may know the route but need the crew to verify a signal. Give factions something they want from one another so the crew can negotiate between them.

| d6 | Goal | Useful leverage | Limitation |
|---:|---|---|---|
| 1 | Keep a settlement supplied | Stores, schedules, local trust | Thin reserve stock |
| 2 | Control a profitable route | Charts and pilots | Needs outside repairs |
| 3 | Recover disputed property | Records and witnesses | Cannot prove ownership |
| 4 | Protect a vulnerable workforce | Solidarity and access | Cannot survive a long shutdown |
| 5 | Conceal a costly mistake | Institutional permissions | Evidence already travelled |
| 6 | Establish a frontier claim | A ship and a plausible map | No reliable local support |

At Waycross, a repair cooperative controls the spare parts the relay keeper needs. A route office can open the departure window but wants proof the trip is safe. Neither can get what it wants alone.

## Clues and changes between sessions

Give important conclusions several leads. A cargo seal, a shift record and a witness can all point to the same diverted shipment. If a lead moves, show that change in play. Between sessions, log confirmed facts, remaining rumours and the next action a faction can take with what it has learned.

## Put languages on the map

Use the ordinary language catalog in Player’s Book Chapter 6 and the system notes in its Appendix A. For each place the crew is likely to visit, record the language used for public access, one used in homes or work crews, and the channel a visitor needs. A transfer office can use Galactic Standard even when the team on the other side of the window prefers Lattice Sign. Describe the posted instructions and available interface before making communication part of the risk.

Give a scene two ways to get its essential information. A passenger might ask the dockmaster, read a bilingual sign or seek a translator library. A clue that depends on an uncommon language needs a reachable speaker, archive or other way to investigate it. Reserve checks for uncertainty, such as a damaged record or whether an agreed gesture means stop or wait.

At the Lattice Moons, a stranded rigger signs through a pressure window. An explorer who knows Lattice Sign understands the warning if they can see it. A translator with the right library can reproduce the literal sign. If neither is available, the rigger can point to the pressure gauge and the crew can try to confirm one emergency signal. That exchange does not teach the crew Lattice Sign.
